Shruti and Sa

Every swara you sing is measured from one note, Sa. You choose where Sa sits to suit your voice, and a drone keeps it sounding while you practise.

Two meanings of shruti

You will hear shruti (also written sruti) used in two ways.

  • Your pitch: the note you have chosen as Sa. “What is your shruti?” means “Where is your Sa?” A shruti box is a small instrument that sounds it for you.
  • The fine steps of pitch: in music theory, a shruti is a very small interval, smaller than the gap between two swaras. Traditional texts count 22 of them in an octave, though musicians have long debated the number and their exact sizes.

For practice, the first meaning is the one that matters: find your Sa and stay with it.

Sa is the note you choose

On a keyboard, C is always the same pitch. In Carnatic music, Sa is not a fixed pitch: it is wherever you put it. A man might sing with Sa at C, a woman with Sa at G, a child higher still, and all three sing the same song in the same raga.

This works because every swara is a fixed distance from Sa. Move Sa and all the other swaras move with it, keeping their distances. The keys you would press on a keyboard change; the swaras and the way the raga sounds do not.

Naming your pitch: kattai

Carnatic musicians often name their Sa by kattai, counting up the keys of a harmonium from C. The white keys are whole numbers and the black keys are halves:

So “5 kattai” means Sa is G. Kattai doesn’t say which octave: a man and a woman can both sing at 5 kattai, an octave apart.

The drone

A drone is a sound that doesn’t change: it keeps Sa (and usually Pa) in your ear the whole time, so you always have something to sing against. The traditional drone is the tanpura (or tambura), a long-necked instrument with four strings that are plucked one after another, over and over. Usually one string is tuned to Pa and three to Sa, one of them an octave lower. Electronic shruti boxes and apps imitate it.

Listen to the drone for a few minutes before you sing. Then, when you sing Sa, you will hear whether it blends with the drone or rubs against it. That blend is what “singing in shruti” means.

Choosing your Sa

A good Sa lets you sing comfortably from the Pa below it to a little above upper Sa: the lessons reach lower Pa, and later songs go a few swaras past upper Sa. Sa itself should feel easy and full, not pressed and not breathy.

  • If your teacher has given you a Sa, use theirs.
  • If upper Sa feels strained, try a lower Sa. If low notes disappear or Sa feels weak, try a higher one.
  • Your Sa can change as your voice develops, but keep one Sa for a while: your ear learns the swaras at that pitch.

The first exercise: Sa, Pa, upper Sa

Before any scale, sing the notes the drone already gives you:

  1. Listen to the drone, then sing Sa. Hold it, relaxed, until it settles into the drone.
  2. Sing Pa, the fifth swara. You can hear it in the drone too.
  3. Sing upper Sa, an octave above Sa. Don’t push: if it strains, choose a lower Sa.
  4. Come back down: upper Sa, Pa, Sa.

When these three notes are steady, every other swara has something to be measured against.

The swara names, R₁, G₃ and the rest, are explained in The seven swaras.

Practise this in Purandara

  • Choose your Sa: pick it by kattai, and fine-tune it to match a teacher’s shruti box.
  • Find my Sa: sing your lowest and highest comfortable notes and get a suggestion.
  • Shruti practice: sing over the tanpura and hold Sa, Pa or upper Sa steady.
