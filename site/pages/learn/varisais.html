The varisai sets

Varisais are the first exercises, all in Mayamalavagowla and Adi tala. Each set trains one thing, and together they take you from a plain scale to clean steps, pairs, leaps and a wider range.

Sarali varisai: figures against the scale

Sarali varisais teach the swaras in order, a few steps at a time. Each one is four avartas long: a short figure, then the plain scale going up; the same figure turned round, then the plain scale coming down. So every exercise returns to the shape you already know, and you hear each new figure against it.

Read through the set and you can hear it being built:

  • The first two go only as far as Ri, then Ga, and turn back to Sa.
  • The next eight keep S R G M at the start and change only what follows: a held Pa, a jump back to Sa, a held Ni, a turn at Pa, a dip to Ma, zig-zags.
  • The last six bring new shapes: a pair sung out of order, then the opening figure starting on Sa, Ri, Ga and Ma in turn, and finally a leap to upper Sa.

Notice where the figures stop: almost never on Sa. They leave the phrase hanging, and the plain scale that follows starts from Sa and brings it home.

Some exercises hold a swara for two beats, written with a comma (P ,). Hold the note through the comma without a gap; these long notes help steady the voice.

Sthayi varisai: further up and down

Sthayi varisais stretch the range. The madhya sthayi set moves in longer figures around the middle octave, with held notes. The tara sthayi set climbs above upper Sa, and the mandra sthayi set goes below your Sa. Before you start the upper and lower sets, check with your teacher that your Sa leaves room for them: a comfortable scale doesn’t mean the wider range is comfortable too.

Janta varisai: pairs

Janta means a pair, as in SS RR GG MM. The first exercise climbs the scale in pairs; the later ones vary the patterns, mixing repeated swaras with steps and leaps. The second swara of a repeated pair is given a slight emphasis; how that is sung is best learned by ear from a teacher. Keep both swaras of a pair exactly on pitch and equally long.

Vakra janta varisais (vakra means crooked) take the pairs in zig-zags, so you have to find each next swara without sliding to it.

In Purandara, Janta 1–6 and both Vakra Janta exercises put two swaras in each written place. Janta 7 instead uses single-swara places and holds: don’t assume all seven have the same notes-per-beat count. See how paired notes affect the speed settings.

Dhattu varisai: leaps

Dhattu means a jump. These exercises leap over a swara and come back, in patterns that climb the scale and come down again. They train the ear and voice to reach a distant swara directly. Skip the swara in between completely: don’t slide through it or sing it softly. If a leap is unclear, sing its two ends slowly a few times, then put it back in the exercise.

The order to learn them

Learn Sarali first, one exercise at a time, in the first speed, then in the second and third (see The speeds). The other sets follow; books and teachers order them a little differently. Purandara follows this order:

  1. Plain scale
  2. Sarali varisai (16)
  3. Madhya sthayi varisai (5)
  4. Janta varisai (7)
  5. Vakra janta varisai (2)
  6. Dhattu varisai (6)
  7. then the alankarams, one in each of the seven talas

Stay with an exercise until it is in tune and in tala at one speed before moving on. There is no prize for finishing the set quickly.

Why the numbers differ between books

Books don’t number the varisais the same way. Some count the plain scale as Sarali 1, so their Sarali 2 is the first exercise here; others start numbering after it. Sets also vary in size: one textbook has ten Janta varisais, and Purandara has seven. When you follow a teacher or a book alongside the app, match the exercises by their notes, not their numbers. In Purandara the plain scale is a set of its own, and Sarali 1 is the first figure (S R S R).
