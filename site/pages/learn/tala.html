Adi tala

A tala is a repeating cycle of beats that you keep with your hand while you sing. The first lessons are in Adi tala, eight beats to a cycle, and the hand shows exactly where in the cycle you are.

Beats and cycles

A tala counts time in beats, called aksharas (or aksharakalas). One trip through the tala, from its first beat back to the first beat again, is an avarta (also avartanam). A lesson or a song is a number of avartas, one after another, and the tala never stops between them.

A Carnatic tala is kept with gestures that differ through the cycle: a beat of the hand, finger counts and waves. You can tell from your hand alone where you are in the avarta, which is what lets you keep your place when the swaras get fast.

The parts of a tala

A tala is built from parts called angas. Three are in everyday use:

AngaWrittenHow you count itBeats
LaghuIA beat of the hand, then finger counts3, 4, 5, 7 or 9
DrutamOA beat, then a wave2
AnudrutamUA single beat1

The drutam and anudrutam are always the same length. A laghu’s length is set by its jati: 4 beats in the commonest (chatusra) jati, and 3, 5, 7 or 9 in the others.

Adi tala: 4 + 2 + 2

For a finger count, touch the fingertip down; for a wave, turn the hand over and bring the back of it down. Written notation puts a single bar after the laghu and a double bar at the end of the avarta, so a line of the first lesson looks like S R G M | P D | N S′ ||.

Adi tala is often compared with 4/4 time, and it has the same eight beats as two bars of 4/4. But its beats group as 4 + 2 + 2, not 4 + 4, and the hand shows that grouping: the second beat of the hand comes on the fifth beat, the waves on the sixth and eighth.

Count first, then sing

  1. Count one full avarta with your hand, saying the beats aloud: “beat, one, two, three, beat, wave, beat, wave”.
  2. Keep counting and sing, one swara on each beat. The swara changes exactly when your hand moves.
  3. If you lose the tala, stop singing but keep your hand going, and come back in at the start of the next avarta.

Keep the hand light and even. It should keep the time for your voice, not follow it: if a phrase is hard, the hand does not wait.

Other talas

Every tala is built from the same three angas. Seven talas, the suladi sapta talas, are the basic set: Dhruva, Matya, Rupaka, Jhampa, Triputa, Ata and Eka. Each is a different arrangement of angas; Adi tala is one form of Triputa (a laghu and two drutams) with a four-beat laghu. Purandara Dasa is credited with setting simple exercises in all seven, and the alankarams are those exercises: one in each tala. That page lists how each tala is counted.

A slow piece can also be counted with two counts to each beat of the hand, called two kalai. The varnams in the app are in Adi tala in two kalai: sixteen counts to the avarta (see Varnams).

Practise this in Purandara

  • Play a varisai: the tala strip above the notation lights each beat (x 1 2 3 x v x v).
  • Tala, count-in and practice: turn on the tala click, where the beat, finger counts and wave each have their own sound, and a count-in of one avarta.
  • Alankarams: the seven talas, one alankaram each.
