The seven swaras

Carnatic music names its notes with seven syllables, the swaras: Sa Ri Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni. Five of them come in more than one form, which gives twelve places in the octave and sixteen names for them.

Sa Ri Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni

Each swara has a full name and a short one. You sing the short names; notation writes just the first letter.

After Ni comes Sa again, an octave higher, and the seven start over.

Two fixed swaras, five that vary

Sa and Pa never change. Sa is the note you choose (see Shruti and Sa) and Pa is always the same distance above it. That is why the drone sounds just these two.

The other five each have more than one form. Ri can be low, middle or high; so can Ga, Dha and Ni; Ma has two forms. Which form a song uses depends on its raga, and the forms are what make one raga sound different from another.

Twelve places, sixteen names

An octave has twelve places for a swara, one for each key of a keyboard octave. Counted from Sa, they are named like this, with the keys when Sa is C:

The small number says which form: R₁ is the lowest Ri, R₃ the highest. Four places have two names. R₂ and G₁ are the same pitch, for example; a raga calls it one or the other so that its scale can name every swara once, in order (Sa, a Ri, a Ga, a Ma, Pa, a Dha, a Ni).

Some books number only the twelve places and give each one name, so they write Ga₂ for Antara Gandharam where this site writes G₃, and Ni₂ for Kakali Nishadam where this site writes N₃. Check which system a book uses before reading its numbers. Purandara and these pages use the sixteen names.

The keys are a guide to where each swara sits, not its exact pitch: against the drone, several swaras sit a few cents away from the keyboard (see Tuning: Just and Equal).

Three octaves

The seven swaras from Sa to Ni make one octave, a sthayi. Your voice mostly uses three:

Madhya sthayi, the middle octave
From your Sa up to Ni. Most of the first lessons stay here.
Tara sthayi, the upper octave
From upper Sa upwards.
Mandra sthayi, the lower octave
The octave below your Sa. The lessons mostly reach its top few swaras, the Pa, Dha and Ni just under Sa.

Notation marks the octave on the swara, but books do it differently:

Going up and coming down

A raga’s scale is given twice: going up, the arohanam, and coming down, the avarohanam. In the raga of the first lessons they are:

  • Arohanam: S R₁ G₃ M₁ P D₁ N₃ S′
  • Avarohanam: S′ N₃ D₁ P M₁ G₃ R₁ S

Some ragas leave swaras out, or take a different path up than down, which is why both are given. More on this raga in Mayamalavagowla.

Practise this in Purandara

  • Play a varisai: start with the plain scale, the seven swaras up and down.
  • Sing along: the pitch lane shows each swara’s line, so you can see where your voice sits.
