Start here

These pages explain the music behind the exercises: what you hear, what you sing, and how to count it. They are written for beginners, and you don’t need to read music.

What the first lessons train

Carnatic music separates the music you practise from the music you perform. The first lessons are practice music (abhyasa gana): short, patterned exercises that build three skills you will use in every song.

Sruti: staying on pitch
Singing in tune with a drone that sounds your Sa, the home note you choose for your voice.
Swara: each note in its place
Knowing the seven swaras and finding each one cleanly, without sliding up to it.
Laya: keeping time
Counting the tala with your hand, and keeping it steady while the swaras get faster.

The graded lessons

The lessons students begin with were set in order by Purandara Dasa in the sixteenth century, and teachers still use them. They are sung in the raga Mayamalavagowla, whose two halves have the same shape, so the scale is easy to learn. Each set adds one thing to the last:

  1. Sarali varisai: short figures against the plain scale, up and down.
  2. Sthayi varisai: figures that reach into the octave above (and, later, below).
  3. Janta varisai: each swara sung twice, cleanly.
  4. Dhattu varisai: leaps over a swara and back.
  5. Alankarams: one figure in each of the seven basic talas.
  6. Gitams: the first songs, with words.
  7. Swarajatis and then varnams: longer pieces that join swaras, words and speed.

Books and teachers order the varisai sets a little differently: some teach Janta and Dhattu before the sthayi exercises, others after. Follow your teacher. Purandara has every set in this order, from a plain scale to start with.

How a practice session goes

  1. Start the drone and listen for a few minutes before you sing. It sounds Sa and Pa; let them settle in your ear.
  2. Sing Sa and hold it, steady and relaxed. Then the swaras one at a time, up to the upper Sa and back.
  3. Count the tala with your hand once the swaras feel secure, and sing the lesson slowly, one swara to each beat.
  4. Get faster only when it is clean. Being in tune and in time matters more than being fast.

A little practice every day does more than a long session once a week.

What a teacher gives you

These pages and the app can help with the plain notes: finding Sa, placing each swara, keeping the tala. A teacher gives you what they can’t. They hear your voice and correct it, set your Sa, show you how each exercise is shaped, and teach the gamakas, the movements that make a raga sound like itself. Use these pages alongside lessons, not instead of them.

Practise this in Purandara

  • Getting started: set your Sa and play your first exercise.
  • Choose your Sa: find a Sa that suits your voice.
  • Play a varisai: follow the notation and the tala.
