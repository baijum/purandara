Mayamalavagowla, the first raga

Almost every Carnatic student begins in Mayamalavagowla. The varisais and alankarams are sung in it, and its scale has a shape that is easy to learn and easy to hear.

Its swaras

Mayamalavagowla uses all seven swaras, one form of each, the same going up and coming down:

  • Up (arohanam): S R₁ G₃ M₁ P D₁ N₃ S′
  • Down (avarohanam): S′ N₃ D₁ P M₁ G₃ R₁ S

In words: Sa, the lowest Ri, the highest Ga, the lower Ma, Pa, the lowest Dha and the highest Ni. (The forms are explained in The seven swaras.) With Sa on C, these are the keys:

A raga with all seven swaras, the same up and down, is a melakarta, a parent scale. There are 72 of them, numbered in a fixed order, and Mayamalavagowla is number 15. Many other ragas take their swaras from it.

The shape of the scale

Count the keys between neighbouring swaras and a pattern appears:

StepS–R₁R₁–G₃G₃–M₁M₁–PP–D₁D₁–N₃N₃–S′
Keys1312131

The lower half, S R₁ G₃ M₁, and the upper half, P D₁ N₃ S′, have exactly the same shape: a small step, a wide step, a small step. A whole step, Ma to Pa, joins them. Once you can sing the lower half, the upper half is the same pattern, starting from Pa. This balance is one reason the lessons begin here.

What to listen for

  • Ri and Dha sit close above Sa and Pa. R₁ is only one key above Sa, and D₁ one key above Pa. They easily drift up towards the next key; keep them close.
  • The wide steps stay wide. R₁ to G₃ and D₁ to N₃ are each three keys, wider than any step in a plain major scale. Let Ga and Ni reach their place; don’t let them sag.
  • Ga to Ma and Ni to upper Sa are small steps, one key each.
  • Sa and Pa are your anchors. Check them against the drone whenever a phrase feels unsure.

A good way in is a little at a time: Sa, Ri, Sa; then Sa, Ri, Ga; then the lower half up and down; then Pa to upper Sa; then the whole scale.

Against the drone

On a keyboard, every key is the same distance from the next. Sung against a tanpura, Mayamalavagowla’s swaras settle a little differently: R₁ and D₁ sit slightly higher than the keyboard’s keys, and G₃ and N₃ slightly lower, by about an eighth of a key. Your ear will find these places from the drone. Tuning: Just and Equal has the exact figures, and lets you choose either in the app.

More than a scale

The plain scale is the raga’s skeleton, not the raga. In songs, Mayamalavagowla’s swaras are shaped with gamakas, small movements and oscillations around a swara, and with phrases that belong to this raga. Those come from listening and from a teacher. The early lessons are sung mostly plain, which is what the app plays and marks.

After the varisais, some teachers take the same exercises into other ragas, such as Shankarabharanam, to train the ear on different swaras.

Practise this in Purandara

  • Play a varisai: the plain scale, then Sarali Varisai, all in Mayamalavagowla.
  • Sing along: see whether Ri and Dha stay close and Ga and Ni reach their place.
  • Tuning: Just and Equal: hear the scale tuned to the drone or to a keyboard.
