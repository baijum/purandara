Singing in tune

Singing in tune is a listening skill before it is a voice skill. You learn to hear where a swara should be, sing it, and notice whether it landed. It grows with calm, regular practice, not with effort.

Listen, echo, check, retry

When a swara is hard to find, slow down and go round this loop:

  1. Listen. Hear the swara once or twice without singing. Let it register before you make a sound.
  2. Echo. Sing it back gently. If a reference is still sounding, keep singing a moment after it stops, on your own.
  3. Check. Did it blend with the drone, or rub against it? A pitch display can confirm what your ear thinks.
  4. Retry. If it missed, stop, listen again, and try once or twice more. Starting afresh works better than sliding around until you find it.

This is how lessons have always been taught: the teacher sings, the student listens and sings it back, again and again.

Using a pitch display

A tuner or a pitch line on a screen is a useful second opinion, as long as your ear stays in charge.

  • Wait for the note to settle. The start of a note is often breathy or scooped; judge the steady middle.
  • Check the octave too. A Sa an octave too high or low is still Sa by name, but not the note you meant.
  • Make sure it is hearing you. A display can pick up the drone or a speaker instead of your voice. Headphones help.
  • Close is good. A beginner’s aim is to land near the swara and hold it steadily. Don’t chase every flicker of the line.
  • A moving line can be right. In songs, gamakas move around a swara on purpose. Steady lines matter for the plain-note lessons.

When your voice slides

First ask whether you can hear the swara clearly. If not, the problem is listening: hear it longer, then sing it after a short pause.

If you can hear it but your voice misses:

  • Use a smaller step. Practise Sa to Ri, or Ri back to Sa, before Sa to Ga.
  • Split a leap into steps you can sing, then take the steps out again.
  • Go back to Sa and Pa from the drone, then try again from there.
  • Sing more softly. Pushing makes pitch harder to control, not easier.

Look after your voice

  • Sing at a comfortable, speaking-level volume. Very loud and very soft singing both strain the voice.
  • Stop if you feel pain, tightness or tiredness in your throat, and rest.
  • Don’t practise when you are hoarse or unwell; rest your voice instead.
  • Drink water, and take short breaks in longer sessions.
  • Breathe whenever you need to, at the end of a phrase rather than in the middle of a word.
  • If hoarseness or a sore, strained throat keeps coming back, see a doctor.

A short practice session

Fifteen minutes on most days does more than an hour once a week. One way to spend them:

MinutesWhat to do
0 to 2Start the drone and listen. Hum Sa gently if you like.
2 to 5Sa, Pa, upper Sa and back: listen, echo, hold each one.
5 to 11Today’s lesson, slowly, with the tala. Use the reference notes while it is new.
11 to 13The same lesson from memory, with only the drone and tala to guide you.
13 to 15Stop and think: one thing that went better, one thing to work on next time.

With only five minutes: one minute listening to the drone, two on Sa and Pa, two on one short pattern. A short, relaxed session is better than a long, tense one.

Practise this in Purandara

  • Shruti practice: watch your voice against the swara lines, and hold Sa, Pa or upper Sa for 10, 20 or 30 seconds.
  • Sing along: each note is marked in tune, close, high or low, judged on its steady middle. Start with Strictness set to Gentle.
  • Harmonium guide off: sing from memory with only the tanpura and the tala.
  • Microphones and headphones: so the app hears your voice, not its own sound.
