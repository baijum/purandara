The speeds

Each lesson is sung at several speeds. The beat of your hand stays the same; what changes is how many swaras fit into each beat.

Same beat, more swaras

For an exercise starting with one swara to each beat, such as the plain scale, the first speed has one swara per beat of the tala. In the second, two swaras share each beat; in the third, four. Each speed doubles the one before, and your hand keeps exactly the same beat throughout.

SpeedSwaras to a beatSwaras in one avarta of Adi talaThe plain scale fills
First18the way up in one avarta, the way down in the next
Second216up and down in one avarta
Third432up and down twice in one avarta
Fourth864up and down four times

Some books call the first three prathama, dvitiya and tritiya kala. In written notation, a line drawn over a group of swaras means they go twice as fast, and two lines four times as fast.

When a written place contains two swaras

The starting point matters. Purandara’s Speed settings 1–4 use 1×, 2×, 4× and 8× the written pace, not a fixed count of individual swaras. In Janta 1–6 and both Vakra Janta exercises, each written place contains two swaras. The settings therefore give 2, 4, 8 and 16 individual swaras per tala beat: eight per beat is already reached at setting 3.

Janta 7 is different: it has single-swara places and holds. A comma continues the previous note, not a new swara; a rest is silent. Holds, rests and mixed note groupings mean a passage need not have one constant count of new notes per beat. See Janta varisais, and compare notes against the tala beats rather than matching a textbook’s speed number to an app setting.

Three speeds or four?

Practise the first three speeds cleanly and steadily. Fourth speed is an additional challenge when ready, or when your teacher asks for it, not a requirement for every learner or every set. Three speeds are a documented teaching target, though sources differ:

  • Sarali, Dhattu (Dattu) and alankarams: the NIOS textbook and Pondicherry University’s music syllabus specify three speeds.
  • Upper (Tara) and lower (Mandra) sthayi: NIOS describes teaching these exercises in three speeds.
  • Janta: Pondicherry University specifies three speeds, while NIOS Book 2 says four. Its notation shows headings for speeds I, II and III, so the intended fourth stage is not clear. Together with the paired notes, this means the wording alone does not tell you which app setting to use.

Karnatik’s general practice advice goes on to eight notes per beat “when you can”. That is a useful extension, not evidence that every set must reach it. Follow your teacher’s counting and practice target; the sources and page references are below.

How fast that really is

How fast you actually sing depends on the speed and on how quickly your hand beats, the tempo, counted in beats a minute. For the plain scale starting at one swara per beat, the same doubling gives:

SpeedAt 30 beats a minuteAt 60 beats a minute
Firstone swara every 2 seconds1 swara a second
Second1 a second2 a second
Third2 a second4 a second
Fourth4 a second8 a second

So the fourth speed at 30 beats a minute is as fast as the third at 60, or the second at 120. That does not make the speeds interchangeable: fitting more swaras between slower hand movements is a different task. If fourth feels too fast, leave it aside; it is not a test you must pass before moving on.

Building up

  1. First speed, until it is in tune. A comfortable tempo is around 50 to 60 beats a minute. Every swara should land in its place against the drone.
  2. Second speed, lowering the tempo if you need to.
  3. Third speed, starting slow, around 30 beats a minute.
  4. Raise the tempo a little at a time, only when the current tempo feels easy and stays in tune.

Optional fourth speed: if you and your teacher choose to add it, start at a slow tempo once the earlier speeds are clean and steady. Third speed at twice that tempo has the same pace of notes, but fourth asks you to fit twice as many between hand movements.

If a speed you are working on falls apart, drop back one speed or lower the tempo. Don’t keep repeating a speed that isn’t working; that practises the mistake.

What to listen for

  • Every swara the same length. In the plain scale’s second speed, the second swara of each pair falls halfway between hand movements; don’t clip it or rush it.
  • The first swara of each group on the beat, and the others evenly between. Sa and Pa will not always fall on the beat of the hand; that is expected.
  • The hand stays steady as the swaras get faster. If the hand speeds up, the tempo has changed, not the speed.
  • Upper Sa stays relaxed, whichever speed you are practising.
  • You can stop and come back in at the start of any avarta without losing the tala.

Clean and in tune comes before fast. If your teacher orders the speeds or tempos differently, do it their way.

Practise this in Purandara

  • At a faster speed: how the player lays out the notation at each speed, with each beat’s swaras together under its mark.
  • Tempo and Speed: tempo from 30 to 120 beats a minute, speed 1 to 4. Fourth remains available if you choose to practise it.
  • Sing along: check that each swara stays in tune as the speed goes up.
