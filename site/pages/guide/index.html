Getting started

Purandara plays the graded Carnatic lessons in tala at your Sa, and listens while you sing. This guide walks through everything it does; to find a word or a setting, use Search the guide at the top of the page list.

Your first practice

  1. Set your Sa. On Home, Your Sa (Shruthi) shows the current Sa, such as “C3 · 1 kattai”. Tap it to choose another. If you don’t know your Sa, use Find my Sa.
  2. Warm up. Tap Play tanpura and hum Sa until it feels settled, or open Shruti practice to see your pitch as you sing.
  3. Play an exercise. Tap Start here to open the plain scale, then tap Play. Listen to one cycle before you sing.
  4. Sing along. Tap the microphone button at the top of the player to have your notes marked. See Sing along.

Finding your way

The app has a few main places: Home, a page for each set, Shruti practice, Find my Sa, Recordings and About. How you move between them depends on the device.

Android
Tap the menu button (three lines) at the top left, or swipe in from the left edge. The menu lists Home, the sets under Varisais and Alankaram & songs (tap a heading to fold it away), then Sa & voice (Shruti practice, Find my Sa, Recordings) and About. On a tablet held sideways the menu stays open beside the page. Back from any of these returns to Home.
iPhone
Three tabs at the bottom. Practise is Home and the sets; Sa holds your Sa, the tanpura, Shruti practice and Find my Sa; Recordings is your recordings. About is the i button at the top of Home.
iPad
A sidebar on the left lists the same places as the Android menu; the chosen one fills the rest of the screen. In split screen, the iPad uses the iPhone’s tabs.

What the set trains, Practise the whole set for the varisai sets, then every exercise with a small line of its melodic shape, a tick once practised, and your best sing-along result.

An exercise counts as practised once you have heard one whole pass of it to the end. Opening it, or stopping during the count-in, doesn’t count.

What’s in the app

59 pieces. The varisais and alankarams are in raga Mayamalavagowla (or, if you choose, another melakarta); the varisais in Adi tala, each alankaram in its own tala. The gitams begin in raga Malahari; the later gitams, the swarajatis and the varnams each have their own raga.

SetExercisesWhat it trains
Plain scale1The seven swaras up and down, settled against the tanpura.
Sarali Varisai16A short figure set against the plain ascent and descent: steady pitch, even timing.
Madhya Sthayi Varisai5Longer figures across the octave; later, teacher-guided studies.
Janta Varisai7Each swara sung as a pair: clean, even repeated notes.
Vakra Janta Varisai2Paired notes in zigzags: finding the next note without sliding.
Dhattu Varisai6Leaps over a note and back: reaching a distant pitch without sliding.
Alankaram7One climbing figure in each of the seven suladi talas (Dhruva, Matya, Rupaka, Jhampa, Triputa, Ata, Eka): keeping the tala as you sing. Talas longer than eight beats wrap onto two rows. More
Gitam9Purandara Dasa’s first songs in raga Malahari, then gitams in Suddha Saveri, Kalyani, Arabhi, Bhairavi and Anandabhairavi, with their words under the swaras. More
Swarajati2Rara Venu Gopa Bala (Bilahari) and Sambasiva Yenave (Khamas): several swaras to a beat, each charanam sung as swaras and then with words. More
Varnam4The first tana varnams in two kalai, with sections at two speeds. More

Purandara is a practice companion, not a teacher. Gamakas, the traditional paired-note emphasis in Janta varisai, and the octave range for the later studies are best learned from a teacher.
