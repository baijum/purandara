Varnams

After the swarajatis come the first tana varnams: the longest pieces yet, and where gamakas are learned. Purandara has Ninnukori in raga Mohanam, Evvari Bodhana in raga Abhogi, Sami Ninne Kori in raga Shankarabharanam, and Jalajaksha in raga Hamsadhwani — all in Adi tala.

Two kalai

A varnam’s Adi tala is counted in two kalai: two counts to each beat, sixteen to the cycle. The tala strip shows the sixteen counts — each beat’s clap, finger count or wave followed by a · second count — and the click ticks sixteen to the cycle, the second count softer. At the default tempo a count takes half a second. The notation has one place for each count, sixteen to a cycle: each place holds a swara, a comma (which holds the swara before it) or an underscore (a silence). A section sung in double speed fits two of its written avartas into each cycle, so its cycles take four rows, each starting with the mark of its first count.

Two speeds

The varnam’s first half — the pallavi, the anupallavi and the muktayi swaram — is sung twice: once in first speed, then again in double (the section list shows it twice, and the second pass plays twice as fast while the tala keeps its beat). The charanam and each chitta swaram are sung in the second speed only, each chitta swaram returning to the charanam. If your teacher orders the speeds differently, sing it their way; the app’s order is the common one and easy to change.

Varnams are long: use Section above the notation to practise one part at a time, and lower the tempo to learn a line.

The ragas

Mohanam climbs S R₂ G₃ P D₂ S′ (no Ma, no Ni) and comes down the same way. Abhogi climbs S R₂ G₂ M₁ D₂ S′ (no Pa, no Ni) and comes down the same way. Shankarabharanam is the 29th melakarta, S R₂ G₃ M₁ P D₂ N₃ S′ both ways. Hamsadhwani climbs S R₂ G₃ P N₃ S′ (no Ma, no Dha) and comes down S′ N₃ P G₃ R₂ S. Sami Ninne Kori’s notation marks a second Ni in places; that reading is left for your teacher to settle.

Gamakas

Varnams are where the gamakas are learned, and the harmonium plays every note plain: shakes, slides and oscillations are up to your voice. Sing-along compares you with the plain note, so a gamaka can be marked high or low even when you sang it your teacher’s way — set Strictness to Gentle, and learn the gamakas from your teacher.

Where the notation comes from

All four varnams follow the notation in Shivkumar Kalyanaraman’s archive. Ninnukori is also checked note by note against Dr. Rose Muralikrishnan’s teaching site. Varnams are taught with small differences between traditions; if yours differs, sing it your teacher’s way. See Credits.
