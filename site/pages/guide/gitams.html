Gitams

Gitams are the first songs: a melody, a tala and words. Purandara has the four pillari gitams in raga Malahari that Purandara Dasa composed for beginners, then Analekara in raga Suddha Saveri, Kamalajadala in Kalyani, Re Re Sri Ramachandra in Arabhi, Sri Ramachandra in Bhairavi, and Kamala Sulochana in Anandabhairavi.

Raga Malahari

Malahari is a janya of Mayamalavagowla. It climbs S R₁ M₁ P D₁ S′ and comes down S′ D₁ P M₁ G₃ R₁ S, and it has no Ni. The gitams also go a little below Sa (Padumanabha reaches lower Dha, written Ḍ) and above upper Sa (upper Ri, R′).

Analekara starts high, around upper Sa, and climbs to upper Ma. If your Sa is very high, the top notes are beyond the app’s harmonium: the player says so and offers the nearest Sa that fits.

A swara with two forms, and a vakra raga

Kamalajadala is in Tisra Triputa — the same seven-beat tala as Analekara and the two Malahari gitams before it — though some traditions sing it as Misra Chapu, a rendering of the same seven beats clapped as three plus four. Sri Ramachandra is in Dhruva tala (fourteen beats), and its Bhairavi sings both Dhaivatas: where the notation shows a small digit after the letter (D₁ beside the raga’s D₂), that note sounds its other form. Kamala Sulochana’s Anandabhairavi climbs in a zigzag (S G₂ R₂ G₂ M₁ P D₂ P N₂ S′), dips below Sa (mandra Ni, written Ṇ), and rises to upper Ga.

Sections and words

  • The Malahari gitams, Analekara and the swarajatis are sung in parts, each starting with a heading: Charanam 1, Pallavi and so on. The refrain (the pallavi) is written out every time it is sung, so the notation always follows the singing. The last three gitams are through-composed — their source writes no sections, so they play as one piece.
  • The words are under the swaras, one syllable per beat. A dash means the syllable before it is held.
  • In Practice settings, Gitam notation chooses what a piece with words shows: Swaras, Words or Both (the default). Learn the swaras first, then sing the words.
  • Screen readers read each avarta's swaras and then its words.

Practise one section

Above the notation, Section: Whole piece lets you choose one part (a charanam, the pallavi…) to practise alone. It plays once, or round and round with Repeat on. Choose Whole piece to go back; only whole-piece plays count as practised.

Singing along, and gamakas

The harmonium plays every note plain, but a gitam is sung with gamakas: some notes are shaken or slid into. Sing-along compares your voice with the plain note, so a gamaka can be marked high or low even when you sang it the way your teacher does. Set Strictness to Gentle for gitams, and learn the gamakas from your teacher.

Where the notation comes from

The swaras and words of the Malahari gitams and Analekara follow karnatik.com, checked note by note against Shivkumar Kalyanaraman’s archive. Sri Gananatha’s refrain follows the archive and the book Sangeeta Bala Padam, Part 1. The last four gitams follow that archive (Kamala Sulochana: a gitam-notes collection and a staff rendering, both supplied by the app’s owner) — single sources, a choice recorded on the project’s issue tracker. Gitams are taught with small differences between traditions; if yours differs, sing it your teacher’s way. See Credits.
